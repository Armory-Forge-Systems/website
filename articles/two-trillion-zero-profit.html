Two Trillion Dollars. Zero Profit. (So Far.) — Signet Article #062

The Forge — Armory Forge Systems — Signet Article #062

On Thursday, Anthropic went public. It priced at $83, opened at $111.50, and ended the day worth roughly two trillion dollars. It raised $81.3 billion — the largest IPO in history, past the record SpaceX set in June.

The same morning, Walmart reported better-than-expected profit and revenue, raised its outlook for the year, and saw its stock drop about three percent.

Walmart is a company that sold $713 billion of goods last year and employs about 2.1 million people. It crossed a one-trillion-dollar valuation in February. Anthropic is now worth about twice that, with 5,300 employees, and has never posted a profitable year.

Zero profit. (So far.)

That parenthetical is the story. Not because the loss is embarrassing — because it tells you what the buyers think they are buying. Nobody paying $111.50 a share believes they are purchasing Anthropic's 2026 income statement. They are buying the version of the economy that exists in 2030, and they are paying today's price for it.

The numbers, honestly

Anthropic's first six months of 2026 show a net loss of $80.7 billion. That number is real, and it is almost entirely not what it looks like. $72.4 billion of it is an accounting charge: as the company's valuation climbed, so did the value of the convertible notes it had sold to investors — IOUs that turn into stock. The rules require booking that increase as a loss. Nobody wrote a check.

Underneath the accounting, the operating story moved. Revenue went from $4.7 billion in the first quarter to $11.6 billion in the second. The cost of the computing power that runs Claude went from $3.4 billion to $5.6 billion — a much smaller jump. In the second quarter the company recorded a profit of roughly $974 million. First quarter of 2025, compute had cost 251% of revenue. By the second quarter of 2026 it was 48%.

Third quarter revenue came in at $17 billion, twelve times the year before. Nine months of 2026 totaled $33.2 billion. For context: all of 2025 was $2.5 billion. All of 2023 was $41 million. The company's own projections, reported out of its investor documents, have it breaking even in 2028.

So: no profitable year yet, a forecast that says one is coming, and a share price that assumes the forecast is conservative.

Why the market is not crazy

Run the division. Walmart generates about $339,000 of revenue per employee. Anthropic, on nine months of 2026, generates about $6.3 million — roughly eighteen times as much, and that is with the year not finished.

A retailer's value scales with stores, trucks, shifts, and people. Every additional dollar of revenue requires more of all four. That is why Walmart is a magnificent machine that grows around five percent a year and has raised its dividend for fifty-three consecutive years. It is not a knock. It is a description of a business whose output is tied to its headcount.

Anthropic's marginal cost of serving the next customer is compute, and compute gets cheaper in public while the model gets better in private. When the cost of your next unit falls, the ceiling comes off. That is what a forty-something multiple on revenue is buying: not today's profit, but the shape of the curve underneath it.

The part almost nobody priced in yet

Here is where the two-trillion number stops being about software.

When Claude Fable 5.2 shipped on 9 November, Anthropic mentioned, almost in passing, that the model had substantially improved at controlling physical robots — including tasks it was never trained on. On RoboDojo, a public benchmark where a model has to drive a simulated two-arm robot from camera images, Fable 5.2 scored 64.9%. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, the model that had set the pace in robotics, scored 29.8%.

There is video of the thing unscrewing a bottle cap and drawing a picture of a robot.

The detail that matters is how that happened. Anthropic's own researchers say the gains in robotics were not the result of a robotics program. They emerged from general improvements in the model — the same training that made it better at code and reasoning. The company had also already run language models against a real Unitree Go2 quadruped, a 29-degree-of-freedom Unitree G1 humanoid, and a robotic arm, from direct motor torque all the way up to writing its own controller code.

And in the last few months, Anthropic confirmed to Reuters that it has built a physical biology laboratory in the Bay Area where Claude directs robotic lab equipment through real experiments with limited human involvement.

Read those three things together. A general-purpose model, with no robotics training, learned to move metal. Then it started running a lab. Meanwhile, inside Anthropic, Claude now writes more than 80% of the code merged into the company's own codebase, and its engineers merge eight times as much code per day as they did in 2024.

At Davos in January, MIT's Daniela Rus reported the state of the art from the other direction: whole fleets of robots already moving shipping containers around the clock with no human intervention — running on purpose-built robotics software.

Two halves of the same future, converging. The purpose-built half is already deployed and cannot generalize. The general half just learned to grab things.

What this means if you run a business

You are not Anthropic, and you are not Walmart. That is exactly why this matters to you.

For thirty years, the way a small company competed with a giant was labor arbitrage and hustle: the giant had scale, you had speed. AI breaks the scale advantage, because the giant cannot buy a better version of Claude than you can. The same 5,300-person company is selling the capability that used to require a department of forty to anyone with a credit card.

Which raises the only question that actually matters for your business: which of your departments is now a subscription?

Not "should we use AI." That decision was made for you by your competitors. The real work is the handoff — the same work Anthropic's own engineers did when they let a model write 80% of their code. You decide which work a machine owns, you give it a name and a territory and a set of permissions, and you hold it to the same standard you would hold a person. Then you do it again for the next department.

That is a strategy, not a tool purchase. It is why the valuation gap exists in the first place.

The parenthetical

Two trillion dollars for a company that has never turned a yearly profit is not a bubble story or a triumph story. It is a forecast, priced. The market looked at a business that sells thinking and said: thinking is about to be most of the economy.

Whether that is right will be settled by robots, not by spreadsheets. The first time a general model picks up something it was never taught to hold, the argument is over.

Keep the "(So Far.)" in mind. It is doing a lot of work — and it is the most honest part of the number.

Anyone can automate a task. Only a strategy lifts the business.

Eleven Days, Three Trillion Dollars, One Man — Signet Article #063

The Forge — Armory Forge Systems — Signet Article #063

On November 12, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive, and board member — effective immediately. No reason given in the release. Eleven days later, on Monday, the same board unanimously voted to put him back, effective immediately. Between those two sentences, roughly eight trillion dollars of stock market value came off, and about three trillion came back.

That is the shape of the story. The argument about it is what matters.

What actually happened

The sequence is documented and not really in dispute:

  • Nov. 8 — The New York Times reported that U.S. government surveillance suggested Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore-based data company Megaspeed, was feeding information to Beijing. That same day Huang wrote to Nvidia employees admitting an extramarital affair, and the board opened an independent investigation, stating it had "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised."
  • Nov. 9 — Trump said he had spoken with Huang, who "had absolutely no idea that the person in question was a spy." Beijing's foreign ministry called the spy claim "completely groundless and pure slander."
  • Nov. 10 — The board retained Joon H. Kim, a former acting U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York, and the firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz. House Foreign Affairs chair Brian Mast said he was prepared to subpoena Huang for a closed-door briefing.
  • Nov. 11 — Reporting added a second relationship, with a Nvidia subordinate.
  • Nov. 12 — Huang resigned. CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO; Harvey Jones became the company's first board chair.
  • Nov. 18 — Nvidia posted the strongest quarter in its history: $115.84 billion in revenue, up about 103% year over year. Kress guided roughly 75% revenue growth for fiscal 2028 and the company expanded its buyback. On that same call, Jones told investors the board had no plans to bring Huang back.
  • Nov. 23 — The board reinstated him anyway, saying it had "received input from senior levels of the United States government" and was confident the return "poses no national security risk." The independent investigation continues. Nvidia is also hiring a chief compliance officer.

The market reaction was not subtle. Nvidia closed Friday at $125.53 and opened Monday up 33% at $167.04, touching $171.50. The S&P 500 gained about 5%, the Nasdaq about 6.5%, the Dow about 3% — Wall Street's best day since April 2025. Huang's net worth rose more than $36 billion in a session. Short sellers lost about $12 billion. CoreWeave and Nebius each jumped more than 30%.

And here is the number that should bother everyone: the rally recovered about $3 trillion of the roughly $8 trillion the S&P 500 lost in the six weeks after its October 6 record. The index is still about $5 trillion below its peak. One CEO's return was worth three trillion dollars, and it still wasn't enough to get back to where the market was seven weeks ago.

On the question of what China got

This is the part where speculation has outrun evidence, in both directions.

What we know: the board said on Nov. 8 it had no reason to believe national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised. CNN, citing U.S. intelligence sources, reported that the intelligence so far "does not prove" Alice Huang was acting as a spy for Beijing. Attorney General Todd Blanche said no conclusions have been reached. The Chinese government denies it. The independent investigation is still running, and Nvidia says it is cooperating with ongoing government inquiries.

What else we know: Megaspeed has been under Commerce Department investigation since late 2024 over alleged diversion of Nvidia chips to China. That case was paused and transferred to the Justice Department after Nvidia intervened. The SEC has opened a civil investigation into whether Nvidia's investor disclosures were misleading, per the Wall Street Journal. On Nov. 19, a House committee disclosed Commerce Department data showing chipmakers requested licenses to sell China 3.4 million AI chips and BIS approved nearly 900,000.

So: no public finding that secrets were stolen. No public finding that they weren't. Multiple live investigations, one of them into whether the company told investors the truth. "We don't know yet" is the honest answer, and anyone telling you otherwise — in either direction — is selling something.

The case for bringing him back

It's stronger than the moralizing coverage suggests.

He is the strategy. Nvidia's roadmap, its supplier relationships, its biggest customer deals, and its standing in Washington were all built around one person over three decades. Analysts were explicit about the premium: Dan Ives put Nvidia at $300 a share with Huang and $225 without. That's not sentiment, that's a quarter of the company.

The transition risk was real and immediate. Nvidia didn't lose a quarter in the scandal — it posted record revenue. But the questions on that call were about succession, not silicon. A company whose entire competitive position rests on shipping a new architecture every year cannot afford a two-year CEO search.

The government effectively cleared him. The board's stated basis for confidence was input from senior U.S. officials. Read plainly, that means the people with access to the actual intelligence did not consider him a national security risk. If the standard is "no evidence of wrongdoing," he meets it.

The personal conduct question is not a board's jurisdiction in the way people want it to be. Affairs are a matter for his family. If the standard for removal is private conduct, that standard has never been applied consistently to founders, and pretending otherwise is theater.

The case against

Also stronger than his defenders admit.

The board's own investigation isn't finished. They reinstated him mid-inquiry. Whatever the independent counsel finds, the board has now pre-committed to living with it. That is not governance, it's an admission that governance was optional.

The conflict of interest is structural, not romantic. Huang has been the loudest voice in Washington arguing to sell AI chips to China. His reported partner ran a company under federal investigation for diverting Nvidia chips to China, and that investigation was paused and handed to DOJ after Nvidia intervened. You do not have to believe anyone was a spy to find that pattern disqualifying in a CEO who sets export policy advocacy.

Political rescue is a precedent nobody should want. Trump says he convinced the board. He called the decision "correct and proper" and Huang "a great patriot and national treasure." Whether or not you like the outcome, the mechanism is now public: a president can phone a board and reverse a resignation in eleven days. Every future CEO scandal at a strategically important company now has a template, and it isn't a legal one.

The investigations don't go away because the stock went up. Congressional testimony requests, a subpoena threat from a committee chair, an SEC disclosure probe, a DOJ inquiry. Huang returns to a job where his calendar now includes depositions, and a new chief compliance officer will be building a function around him rather than above him.

"No national security risk" is narrower than "no problem." The board was told his return doesn't endanger the country. Nobody said it doesn't endanger the company.

The thing both sides are quietly agreeing on

Strip out the politics and the personality, and Monday was a stress test that Nvidia failed in an instructive way: a company worth more than most countries' economies has no succession plan anyone believes in, and the market will pay any price to avoid finding out.

Eight trillion dollars left AI stocks in six weeks without a single bad earnings report. Three trillion came back on a personnel announcement. That is not confidence in Nvidia. That is a market that has stopped pricing companies and started pricing individuals — and it is doing it while eight of the ten largest U.S. companies, 40% of the S&P 500, are tech.

Ben's take — that this man is a serious liability and also beloved — isn't a contradiction. It's the actual finding. Both things are true at once, and the reason they're both true is that we've allowed one person to become load-bearing infrastructure.

What a business owner should take from it

Three things, and none of them require you to have an opinion about Jensen Huang.

Know your key-person risk and write it down. If your biggest customer, your best engineer, or you personally disappeared for eleven days, what happens to revenue? If the answer is "eight trillion dollars," you don't have a company, you have a bet. If the answer is "we'd be fine but slow," you have a company.

Separate the compliance function from the person it's protecting. Nvidia is hiring its first chief compliance officer in the middle of this. That's a corrective action, not a control. Build the function before you need it, and make sure it reports to the board, not the founder.

Don't let a single relationship be your strategy. Whether that relationship is a customer, a supplier, a model provider, or a person. Concentration feels like efficiency right up until the day it feels like an outage.

The market decided Monday that Nvidia is Jensen Huang. It might even be right. But a business that can only be run by one person isn't a business — it's a personality with a balance sheet, and the personality gets a vote.
